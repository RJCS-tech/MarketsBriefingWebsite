# Daily Brief — Friday, 4 September 2026

## Top story: jobs day arrives with the Fed on a knife-edge, and the Iran war keeps widening
Today is the single most-watched data point standing between here and the September 16-17 FOMC meeting: August's official US nonfarm payrolls, due at 8:30am ET, following July's shock contraction and Wednesday's weak ADP print (+38,000 vs +47,000 expected). Consensus estimates cluster around +53,000, but after two straight downside surprises the bar is arguably lower than the headline number suggests — a another weak print would likely tip the Fed back toward a cut, while a solid beat plus this week's oil-driven inflation impulse keeps a hike in play. Layered on top: the US-Iran conflict kept escalating overnight into Thursday, with Iran's Health Ministry reporting 105 civilians injured (including 44 minors) after a US strike near a wedding venue in Sirik, and Tehran retaliating with missile and drone strikes that Kuwait's air defenses intercepted, alongside strikes attributed to Iran against UAE targets. Trump has repeated that the renewed fighting "will not last too long," but markets shrugged off the geopolitics Wednesday — Dow, S&P 500 and Nasdaq all closed roughly 0.5-0.6% higher — while bond markets did not: the 10-year Treasury yield hit 4.82%, its highest since November 2023, on a broad global bond selloff.

## Stock market & economy
Wednesday's close: S&P 500 +0.5% to 7,666.60, Nasdaq +0.5% to 26,217.83, Dow +0.6% to 53,061.95. Earnings did the heavy lifting — Dell Technologies surged 15.8% on adjusted EPS of $7.04 versus $4.97 expected, GitLab jumped 10% on non-GAAP earnings 33% above forecasts, and Snowflake's blowout Tuesday print (revenue +35% y/y, guidance raised) is still rippling through enterprise software sentiment. Not everyone had a good week: Victoria's Secret and NetApp both disappointed. Energy was the strongest sector (+1.3%) as WTI rose to $91-93 and Brent pushed above $97 on the Iran escalation, while seven of eleven S&P sectors actually finished lower, a reminder that the rally is narrower than the index-level numbers suggest.

The bigger story may be in Europe: eurozone inflation jumped to 3.3% in August from 2.9% in July — the highest in nearly three years — driven almost entirely by energy, with ECB economists attributing roughly 90% of the recent energy-inflation increase to the Middle East supply shock rather than demand. Markets are now pricing a near-certain ECB hike on September 10, taking the deposit rate from 2.25% to 2.50%, its second hike this cycle after June's move off zero-ish policy. That's a live, dated catalyst for the EUR-denominated side of a portfolio (IAEX, EUEA): a hike this soon after June suggests the ECB is genuinely worried about a second inflation wave, which is a different, more hawkish posture than markets had priced as recently as August. Gold, meanwhile, is trading around $4,470/oz, up modestly on hopes the Iran re-escalation stays contained — a shift from earlier in the week, when gold had been falling because rising Fed-hike odds were seen as the dominant force. That flip is itself informative: it suggests the market's read on "which fear wins" (geopolitical vs. rate) is genuinely unsettled right now, which likely means more volatility around today's jobs number than usual.

Bottom line for the portfolio: today's payrolls print and next Thursday's ECB decision are back-to-back, dated catalysts that could move both the USD-unhedged (IWDA, VUSA, VHYL) and EUR-denominated (IAEX, EUEA) legs of the portfolio in opposite directions depending on which surprises more.

## Also in the news
The Strait of Hormuz conflict is reshaping global trade flows well beyond oil tankers. Vessels are increasingly rerouting around the Cape of Good Hope — adding 10-14 sailing days — pushing sea freight costs on India-focused lanes up 30-50% (with emergency conflict surcharges of $2,000-4,000 per container) and air cargo rates on India-Middle East routes up 250-300%, with freighter capacity booked out weeks ahead. India's trade deficit has nearly doubled year-over-year to $27.1bn and the rupee has weakened to about 92.3/USD. Separately, "shipping stocks at decade highs" is now itself becoming a talked-about crowded trade (VLCC tanker earnings are up 467% since the crisis began), which is worth knowing if you're tempted to chase it at this point rather than earlier in the cycle.

## Off the beaten path: air cargo and freight-handling as the second-order Hormuz trade
Tankers and marine war-risk insurers (flagged in this brief on prior days) are now well-picked-over trades. A less obvious beneficiary of the same disruption is the air-cargo and freight-handling layer that absorbs shipments diverted off the sea route entirely. Singapore's SATS Ltd (SGX: S58) — one of Asia's largest air-cargo handlers, operating at 14 hubs among the world's top 30 — just reported record Q1 FY27 cargo volumes of 2.59 million tonnes (+8.6% y/y) and cargo revenue up 14.4% y/y to S$893.1m, with management explicitly citing "geopolitical rerouting" as airlines redirect shipments through its network. India's Blue Dart Express (NSE: BLUEDART), the country's largest integrated air-express operator with its own freighter fleet via Blue Dart Aviation, sits at the center of the India-Gulf air-freight squeeze described above, though no confirmed earnings read-through for it specifically has surfaced yet.

The appeal is the same asymmetry logic as the insurance trade: these firms earn on volume and mix-shift toward premium (air) freight regardless of who "wins" the shipping lane, rather than being exposed to spot tanker rates directly. The risks are real too: SATS itself flagged that Middle East disruption is also compressing its EBITDA margins (-0.9pp to 17.3%) via network adjustments and inflation, cargo demand is inherently cyclical and would normalize quickly if the conflict cools, and — as with every trade in this brief tied to the Iran war — a real ceasefire would likely unwind the premium pricing fast. SATS trades in SGD, Blue Dart in INR, both currency considerations for a euro-based portfolio.
