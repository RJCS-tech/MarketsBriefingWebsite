Daily brief Tuesday, 15 September 2026 · 4 min read

10-year Treasury tops 5% for the first time since 2007 as the Fed's two-day meeting opens and the Hormuz diplomatic track collapses

The benchmark 10-year Treasury yield jumped more than 6 basis points to 5.025% on Monday, its highest closing level since 2007, as traders locked in expectations for a Fed rate hike this week rather than the cut that was still plausible a month ago. The Fed's two-day meeting opened today, with a decision due Wednesday, 16 September; CME FedWatch and prediction markets now put the odds of a 25bp hike near 85-90%, up from roughly 19% for a hold as the alternative. The driver is a stack of hot inflation data — August producer prices up 0.4% month-on-month and 5.4% year-on-year, core CPI up a faster-than-expected 0.3% — arriving at the same time as oil, not falling, which is exactly the combination that forces a central bank's hand.

That oil backdrop got worse rather than better overnight. The Omani-brokered meeting in Salalah, where Iran and Gulf Cooperation Council foreign ministers were meant to finalize a temporary Strait of Hormuz shipping arrangement, was postponed indefinitely on Sunday after Saudi Arabia objected that the draft wording would lock in a new status quo it found unacceptable, and Bahrain said it would not attend at all. No new date has been set. That leaves both of the region's oil bypass routes still degraded — Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline remains offline after last week's drone strikes, and Houthi forces retain effective control of the Bab el-Mandeb strait after seizing Mocha and Perim island — with the one diplomatic track that might have eased the pressure now stalled rather than advancing. Brent settled near $108.34/bbl Monday (+3.6%), and WTI was near $102.81 (+1.4%) early Tuesday, both holding just above the psychologically important $100 mark.

02Stock market & economy

Monday's cash session closed lower across the board: the S&P 500 fell 0.48% to 7,619.98, the Nasdaq Composite dropped 0.56% to 26,186.41, and the Dow slipped 0.29% to 52,421.20. The damage was concentrated in chips rather than spread evenly — the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index tumbled 5.9%, with Intel down 5.6-5.7%, Micron and AMD each off roughly 5%, Broadcom down 3.4%, and Nvidia down 2-3.4% depending on the print. The trigger was the same AI-pacing essay flagged in yesterday's brief: Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei's call to slow frontier AI development, echoed publicly by OpenAI's Sam Altman and xAI's Elon Musk, has investors newly worried that a policy-driven slowdown could crimp the AI-infrastructure demand that's been propping up chip valuations all year. Gold, which would ordinarily catch a bid from both the Gulf escalation and the equity selloff, instead eased to around $4,298/oz — the hawkish-Fed narrative continues to outweigh the safe-haven case, the same dynamic that has capped gold for weeks now.

For the unhedged USD sleeve (MSCI World ETF, S&P 500 ETF, High Dividend Yield ETF), Wednesday's Fed decision is now the single most consequential event of the month, arriving on top of — rather than instead of — the AI-safety overhang that already hit the mega-cap tech names those funds are heaviest in; High Dividend Yield ETF's value and energy tilt remains the natural offset if the oil rally holds. For AEX index ETF and EURO STOXX 50 ETF, Shell's direct leverage to firmer crude is again the read-through, while a 25bp Fed hike would also tend to support the dollar against the euro, a modest tailwind for the unhedged funds' EUR-translated returns and a modest headwind for European exporters more broadly.

03Also in the news

Russia-Ukraine diplomacy took a small step forward without resolving anything: Trump and Putin held a phone call after last week's Moscow and Kyiv shuttle trips by envoys Witkoff and Kushner, with Washington describing "new ideas" and a roadmap for a further meeting, possibly in Abu Dhabi, that would now realistically happen no earlier than October. The core obstacle is unchanged — Russia wants Ukrainian withdrawal from the parts of Donbas it claims as a precondition for talks, which Kyiv rejects — and North Korea's Kim Jong Un used a letter to Putin to reaffirm support for the war and hint at further troop deployments, a reminder the conflict still has outside sponsors actively reinforcing rather than de-escalating it.

Not every reaction to the AI-safety story was negative for equities: cybersecurity names rose broadly on Monday even as chips fell, with Palo Alto Networks up more than 4% and CrowdStrike up 5.4%, as investors read heightened AI-risk rhetoric as a signal that security spending — on both traditional infrastructure and AI-specific safeguards — is more likely to rise than fall from here.

04Off the beaten path

cybersecurity stocks as the AI-safety story's quiet beneficiary

Most of Monday's AI-safety coverage focused on who got hurt — chipmakers, on the theory that a deliberate industry slowdown would eventually show up in capex. Less discussed is the trade on the other side: Amodei's proposal explicitly calls for embedding independent, "employee-like access" evaluators inside frontier labs and pushing common industry safety standards, and if that framework gains any regulatory traction, it reads directly across to demand for third-party security and monitoring tooling — the same category cybersecurity vendors already sell into every other regulated industry. Palo Alto Networks and CrowdStrike's Monday gains (roughly +4% and +5.4% respectively) look like the market pricing exactly that connection in real time, alongside a more conventional read that heightened fear of "rogue AI agents" simply raises the near-term case for buying more security software generally.

The setup is speculative rather than confirmed: no legislation or binding industry standard exists yet, Monday's move could just as easily reverse if the AI-pacing story fades from headlines within a week, and cybersecurity stocks already carry rich valuations after a strong 2026 run, meaning a lot of good news may be priced in before any AI-safety-driven demand actually materializes. None of Palo Alto Networks or CrowdStrike carry meaningful weight in MSCI World ETF, S&P 500 ETF, or High Dividend Yield ETF, so this sits outside the existing portfolio entirely — a "watch whether the AI-pacing debate produces any actual policy" item, not a position to take today.
