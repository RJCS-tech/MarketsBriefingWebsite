Daily brief Thursday, 24 September 2026 · 6 min read

The 10-year Treasury yield breaks above 5.1% for the first time since 2007 as hot PMIs, rebounding oil and a hawkish Fed governor lift October hike odds

The bond market delivered the move it had been threatening all month. On Wednesday the 10-year Treasury yield climbed as high as about 5.12-5.13%, its highest level since July 2007, after spending the first half of the week just below 5%. The 5-year yield touched 5% for the first time since 2007, and the 30-year jumped roughly 11 basis points to about 5.4%, its highest intraday level since 2007 and its highest close since 2004. Three things drove it at once. First, S&P Global's flash PMIs came in hotter than expected on every measure: business activity grew at its fastest pace since July 2021, and the manufacturing PMI jumped to 57 against a consensus of 53.6. Second, oil snapped its five-day losing streak (covered below), bringing the inflation worry straight back. Third, Fed Governor Michael Barr said "further policy adjustments are likely to be needed" to get inflation back to target. Barr is one of the voices markets had treated as less hawkish, so that line landed hard.

Rate expectations moved accordingly. CME FedWatch odds of another hike at the late-October meeting rose to roughly 66-78% depending on the time of day, up from about 55% a week earlier, and markets now also put better-than-even odds on a December move. The real-economy effects are showing up quickly: average 30-year US mortgage rates rose 9 basis points to 7.26%, close to a two-year high. The dollar held near two-month highs. Gold was flat at around $4,319/oz. With 10-year real yields this high, holding gold costs more, and that is roughly offsetting the demand for it as a geopolitical hedge.

02Stock market & economy

Equities gave back the week's chip rally. The S&P 500 fell 0.75% to 7,706.03, the Nasdaq Composite dropped 1.13% to 26,936.04, a day after its record close, and the Dow lost 352 points, or 0.68%, to 51,511.59. Rate-sensitive small caps did worse, with the Russell 2000 among the weakest indices. Losses were concentrated in long-duration tech, including Nvidia and AMD, and in financials, which were hit by a separate AI-disruption scare covered below. Oil rebounded after Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian told the UN General Assembly that Iran "will not surrender" to the United States. The US delegation walked out at the start of the speech. Pezeshkian also said Tehran still backs diplomacy, and indirect US-Iran contacts continued in New York. Even so, the market read the day as a step back from Tuesday's optimism. Brent rose about 3-4% to settle back above $100/bbl, around $102-103, and WTI gained 1.8% to $92.16. European markets closed before most of the US selloff: the Euro Stoxx 50 edged up 0.08% to about 6,323, and the AEX outperformed with a 0.78% gain to around 1,111. In Asia on Thursday the Nikkei rose 1.3% on tech-component demand. Mainland China closes Friday for the Mid-Autumn Festival.

Xi Jinping arrived in Washington on Wednesday, and the summit's main economic result was announced before the leaders even met. After meeting Vice Premier He Lifeng, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said on Fox News that the two sides had agreed to extend the Busan tariff truce by two months, from 10 November to 10 January. He said that could lead to "a larger economic package" by then, or the current deal could simply be rolled over again. That is shorter than the six months Bessent had reportedly offered last weekend and far short of Beijing's two-year ask. It removes the November cliff but keeps tariff risk on the calendar for early 2027. Today brings the White House arrival ceremony, bilateral talks focused on trade, rare earths and AI guardrails, and a state dinner. Few desks expect a structural breakthrough.

For the unhedged USD sleeve (MSCI World ETF, S&P 500 ETF, High Dividend Yield ETF), Wednesday was a reminder that the AI-led rally is now running directly into the rate path. A 10-year yield above 5% raises the discount rate on the long-dated earnings that justify mega-cap tech valuations, and those names make up the largest weightings in MSCI World ETF and S&P 500 ETF. High Dividend Yield ETF has less of that duration exposure, but its heavy financials, insurance and telecom weightings took the brunt of the Muse selloff. Its energy holdings helped as oil rose. For AEX index ETF and EURO STOXX 50 ETF, the picture is more mixed. The AEX's outperformance partly reflects Shell rebounding with crude, but higher oil and a firmer dollar also add to the imported inflation that forced the ECB to raise its deposit rate to 2.5% on 10 September, with eurozone inflation at 3.3%. Rising US long-end yields also tend to pull Bund yields up with them, tightening European financial conditions whatever Frankfurt decides.

03Also in the news

Russia-Ukraine diplomacy made some incremental progress at the UN. After meeting Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov in New York on Wednesday, Secretary of State Marco Rubio said both Ukraine and Russia have "voiced an interest" in a limited ceasefire covering energy and grain-related targets. That builds on Zelensky's statement after Tuesday's meeting with Trump that Kyiv is ready to stop striking Russian energy facilities if Moscow does the same, and his call for a trilateral meeting with Putin "as quickly as possible." The caution is that Moscow itself has given no public sign of agreeing to an energy or maritime truce, and it was not clear whether Rubio was reporting real movement from the Russian side. No date has been set for the next trilateral round, and the core dispute over Donetsk remains unresolved. For European portfolios, an energy truce ahead of winter would matter mostly through gas prices and would be a modest positive for EURO STOXX 50 ETF.

Meta used its Connect event on Wednesday to launch hardware for its Muse agent. The main product is the Muse Charm, a keychain-sized device with a 2-inch OLED screen, 5G modem and camera that lets users talk to Muse without unlocking a phone. It ships in December. Meta also unveiled a third-generation Ray-Ban Meta, audio-only smart glasses and new VR glasses. Asian component suppliers rallied on the news overnight.

04Off the beaten path

the "consumer inertia" trade, and why AI agents are hitting banks and insurers

Yesterday's brief looked at how AI agents like Muse benefit CPU makers. Wednesday showed the other side: which businesses investors think agents could hurt. Expedia fell about 7%, Airbnb about 6% and Booking about 5% as investors weighed the prospect of an agent booking a flight or hotel directly, without the user ever visiting an aggregator. The selling spread well beyond travel. The S&P 500 Financials index fell as much as 2.4% to its lowest level since July. JPMorgan, Morgan Stanley and Wells Fargo each lost more than 2.5%, Allstate and Charles Schwab fell more than 5%, and in Europe, telecoms such as Orange and BT fell around 4%. The idea behind these moves is what Goldman Sachs calls "consumer inertia": many companies earn a meaningful share of their profits from customers who never compare prices, never cancel unused subscriptions and never call to renegotiate. Goldman's at-risk basket reads like a list of those business models: AT&T, T-Mobile, Allstate, Progressive, Netflix, Paramount Skydance, Expedia and Booking. If an agent can shop around for your insurance renewal or your phone plan every year without you lifting a finger, that inertia premium shrinks.

This matters for a diversified portfolio more than it might seem. High-dividend funds like High Dividend Yield ETF are overweight exactly the mature, cash-generative businesses in banking, insurance and telecoms whose steady margins partly depend on sticky customers. The AEX has its own concentration in banks and insurers too. The honest caveat is that nothing has actually happened to these companies' revenues yet. Muse has about 2.5 million US downloads in two weeks, which is impressive for an app but tiny next to the customer bases of the firms being sold. There is no data yet on cancellations or plan switches made through agents. The market is pricing a theoretical threat before any evidence arrives, which is what it did with software stocks in earlier waves of AI anxiety. The signal worth watching is whether insurers or telecoms start mentioning higher churn or price-shopping on their next earnings calls. Until then, this is sentiment, not fundamentals.
