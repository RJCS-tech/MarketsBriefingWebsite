Daily brief Thursday, 3 September 2026

Oil back above $95, tanker traffic through Hormuz collapsing

Fresh US-Iran fighting escalated overnight: Trump announced "large and powerful" strikes on Iranian positions after Iran allegedly tried to plant sea mines and struck a US base in Jordan; Iran has pledged retaliation, and Kuwait, Jordan and Bahrain intercepted missiles and issued alerts Wednesday morning. Brent jumped to $95.47 and WTI to $90.72 — both benchmarks' strongest one-day move in over a month — before easing back to $89.71 by the US close as some of the immediate panic faded. The more telling number is traffic: vessel crossings through the Strait of Hormuz fell from 23 the previous Wednesday to just 10 on Monday and 5 on Tuesday, a fifth of normal flow through a route that carries roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil. Despite that, US equities actually firmed: Dow +0.53%, S&P 500 +0.20%, Nasdaq +0.03%, Russell 2000 +0.75% — a sign markets are starting to look past the daily headline noise even as the 10-year Treasury yield pushed to 4.81%, its highest since November 2023.

Stock market & economy

Snowflake blew past estimates after Wednesday's close — Q2 revenue $1.55bn (+35% y/y) versus $1.48bn expected, adjusted EPS $0.62 versus $0.45 expected, and raised full-year guidance to $6.1bn product revenue with operating margin lifted to 14.5%. The stock jumped over 22% in extended trading, a strong signal that enterprise AI-infrastructure demand is still intact even amid the "AI agents will disrupt SaaS" narrative. Broadcom, Palo Alto Networks and HPE round out this week's tech earnings, with Broadcom seen as the key AI-capex read-through.

Labor data continues to soften: ADP private payrolls rose just 38,000 in August, below the 47,000 estimate and the weakest print since January. That sets up Friday's official August nonfarm payrolls (following July's shock -23,000) as the single most important data point before the September 16-17 FOMC meeting; August CPI on September 10 is the other major input. Fed rate-hike odds for September, which jumped from roughly 35% to 57-68% after Chair Warsh's hawkish Jackson Hole speech, are now essentially a coin flip to slightly favor a hike, per CME/Kalshi/Polymarket pricing — a combination of sticky inflation (3.7% 12-month PCE) and the fresh oil-driven inflation impulse from the Iran conflict. Gold, which would normally catch a safe-haven bid on this kind of escalation, has instead drifted down to around $4,357-4,377/oz, because traders read "higher oil → higher odds of a Fed hike" as the dominant force, and a hike is bad for a non-yielding asset like gold. That's a useful tell on how markets are currently weighing the two competing narratives (geopolitical fear vs. rate fear).

Bottom line for the portfolio: rising yields are still the dominant cross-asset force, oil is a second-order but real inflation risk via the Hormuz chokepoint, and Friday's payrolls print is the next scheduled catalyst that could move the September Fed decision meaningfully in either direction.

Off the beaten path — Lloyd's specialty insurers riding the war-risk premium spike

Most of the obvious "Hormuz trade" ideas — tanker owners, oil majors, defense primes — are already crowded and were flagged in this brief on prior days. A less obvious beneficiary sits one layer removed: the Lloyd's of London specialty and marine war-risk insurers who underwrite the cover that shipowners now need just to sail the Gulf. War-risk premiums for a single Hormuz transit have reportedly climbed into the double-digit millions of dollars, and Beazley announced a $1bn Marine War consortium in April specifically to support Hormuz transits — a new, currently very in-demand product line. Lancashire Holdings (LSE: LRE), a smaller, higher-beta specialty (re)insurer, posted a strong H1 2026 with a 90.8% combined ratio and flagged its war-exposure book as a growth driver in its own investor commentary.

The appeal is that this trade is somewhat asymmetric to the tanker trade: insurers collect premium income regardless of whether ships ultimately sail (reduced Hormuz traffic just means fewer, more expensive policies, not zero revenue), and their earnings aren't directly exposed to freight-rate volatility the way shipowners are. The risk side is real, though: specialty insurers carry tail risk if a major vessel loss actually occurs (a large claim could wipe out a lot of premium income at once), reported combined ratios can be volatile quarter to quarter, and — as with the Japan-bank trade flagged Tuesday — any resolution of the Iran conflict would likely compress these premiums quickly, since they're priced for an acute, live crisis rather than a structural shift. Both LRE and Beazley (LSE: BEZ) are London-listed and GBP-denominated, so there's a currency dimension for a euro-based portfolio to consider as well.
